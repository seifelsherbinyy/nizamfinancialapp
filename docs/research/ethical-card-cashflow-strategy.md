# Ethical Credit Card and Cashflow Strategy for HSBC Loan Preparation in Egypt

## Executive summary

The safest and most effective strategy is **not** to get clever with circular credit use. It is to run a **buffered avalanche plan with utilization control**: keep every payment on time, stop using any card that already carries a revolving balance, route new spending either through debit or through a separate card that is always paid in full, reduce reported utilization before the statement closes, and build a small cash buffer while you steadily shrink the most expensive debt first. This approach best fits the stated objective of improving HSBC loan eligibility **without misrepresentation or unsafe borrowing**, because HSBC’s public loan pages emphasize income, employment stability, and approved-employer status, while Egypt’s credit-reporting framework and local bank education materials emphasize payment history, outstanding debt, and credit mix. HSBC’s public pages do **not** disclose a published minimum score or a public debt-to-income cutoff, and iScore itself states that lenders—not the bureau—make the final credit decision under their own policies. citeturn2view0turn18view2turn14view0turn18view1

The key practical conclusion is this: **a 55–56 day interest-free loop only works when the card is paid in full and on time**. HSBC Egypt’s card terms say the maximum interest-free period is up to 56 days from purchase **if you pay the whole balance in full and on time**; if you do not pay the full outstanding amount by the due date, interest is charged from the transaction date based on the daily outstanding balance. That means “debit-credit-debit rotation” is a useful liquidity tool **only for a full-pay card**, not for a card already being revolved. If you revolve and keep spending on that same card, the float becomes expensive very quickly. citeturn3view1turn3view4turn4view1

For HSBC loan preparation specifically, the cleanest three-month signal is: **no late payments, no over-stretching, lower revolving balances relative to limits, stable documented income, and if you already have an installment obligation, three consecutive on-time payments**. If you do **not** already have an installment, opening a new costly installment plan purely for appearances is usually inferior to lowering expensive revolving debt. A small **genuine** 0% merchant installment for a necessary planned purchase can be reasonable only when the total fee is truly low and the monthly installment fits the budget comfortably; HSBC’s own partner pages show that some “0%” plans still carry meaningful administrative fees, so headline rate alone is not enough. citeturn18view1turn14view0turn30view0turn29search1

## How HSBC Egypt and Egyptian credit scoring shape the strategy

HSBC Egypt’s public personal-finance pages show two key loan paths. For **personal finance with salary or installment transfer**, HSBC publicly lists a minimum fixed gross monthly income of **EGP 10,000**, completion of probation with the current employer, age **21 to 65** by the end of repayment, and an employer on the bank’s approved list. For **personal finance without salary or installment transfer**, HSBC again lists a minimum fixed gross monthly income of **EGP 10,000**, but requires at least **one year** with the current employer and offers a shorter repayment period than the salary-transfer version. HSBC’s public loan pages also require recent income documentation and, for salary-transfer products, a transfer letter in the bank’s standard format. citeturn2view0

What those public pages **do not** tell you is equally important: they do not publish a numeric credit-score threshold, a required utilization ratio, or an exact DTI ceiling. That is consistent with iScore’s own explanation that the bureau supplies information and scoring, but the **approval or rejection decision belongs to the bank or lender** according to its credit policy and Central Bank of Egypt instructions. So the right target is not “game the number,” but “improve the signals a prudent lender is likely to care about.” citeturn18view2turn2view0

Egypt’s credit-bureau ecosystem clearly makes those signals visible. iScore states that the Egyptian credit score is designed for the Egyptian market, ranges from **300 to 850**, and summarizes the borrower’s full credit record into a risk indicator. Its explanation of score construction says past payment pattern, debt outstanding relative to limits, credit-history length, and credit types used all matter, and that higher debt compared with available credit lines implies greater repayment risk. A CIB educational explainer on Egypt’s iScore/FICO framework adds that payment history and outstanding debt are the largest factors in score construction for Egyptian consumers, with smaller effects from history length, new credit activity, and credit mix. citeturn18view0turn18view1turn14view0

That leads to a straightforward implication for this goal. If you want to look safer to HSBC over the next three months, you want your file to show: **regular on-time payments**, **no missed minimums**, **lower revolving balances versus limits**, **few or no fresh credit applications**, and **stable income evidence**. CIB’s explainer warns that recent unpaid debt, many late payments, and frequent new applications are negative factors, while iScore’s official material says higher debt versus limits means higher risk. HSBC’s own card terms also state that if you miss the minimum payment, your creditworthiness with HSBC and other banks may be negatively affected, and the bank may inform a credit bureau. citeturn14view0turn18view1turn3view3

HSBC’s card economics make carrying debt unusually expensive in practice. Its current tariff sheet lists a **4.42% monthly finance charge** for Visa Evolution, Visa Platinum, and Visa Cashback cards, and **4.00% monthly** for Premier Mastercard. A monthly rate of 4.42% compounds to roughly **68% effective annual cost** if a balance keeps revolving; even 4.00% monthly compounds to roughly **60% effective annual cost**. That is why the highest-value action in most cases is reducing revolving card debt rather than searching for cosmetic credit-building maneuvers. citeturn7view1turn7view2

There is one public inconsistency worth flagging. HSBC’s current Cashback FAQ states a **2.95% monthly interest rate** for that product, while HSBC’s broader tariff page lists **4.42%** for Visa Cashback. Because those public pages conflict, the safest assumption is to rely on the latest tariff sheet **and your own latest statement** before taking action. Do not build a plan on an older FAQ if your statement or tariff shows a higher rate. citeturn19view0turn7view1turn7view2

## Formula library and worked examples

Below is a spreadsheet-ready formula set built to answer the specific problem: how to close balances slowly, preserve living expenses and a small amount of leisure, use an interest-free loop only where safe, and improve lender-facing reliability.

**Debt-to-income ratio**

**Formula:**  
`DTI = Total required monthly debt payments / Gross monthly income`

**Purpose:**  
A lender-side affordability measure. CFPB defines DTI as monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. HSBC Egypt does not publish a public DTI cutoff, so this is best used as **your own guardrail**, not as a guessed HSBC rule. citeturn10search3turn2view0

**Inputs:**  
Required installment payments, required card minimums, any overdraft or other monthly debt obligations, gross monthly income.

**Worked example:**  
If gross monthly income is **EGP 24,000**, an existing installment is **EGP 1,000**, and a card balance is **EGP 18,000**, a rough minimum using HSBC’s 5% rule for purchases is about **EGP 900**.  
`DTI = (1,000 + 900) / 24,000 = 7.9%`  
If you add a new installment of **EGP 800**, DTI becomes **11.3%**. The math is simple; what matters is that you do **not** guess that HSBC will like a higher DTI just because payments are current. citeturn4view1turn10search3

**Revolving utilization ratio**

**Formula:**  
`Single-card utilization = Statement balance / Card limit`  
`Aggregate utilization = Sum of statement balances / Sum of card limits`

**Purpose:**  
A proxy for how stretched revolving credit is. iScore’s official explanation says higher debt relative to limits increases risk. FICO education likewise says lower revolving utilization is generally better, and a common lender heuristic is to keep utilization at or below roughly **30%**, with materially lower levels often better. HSBC Egypt does not publish a utilization target, so **30% is a practical rule of thumb, not an HSBC rule**. citeturn18view1turn11search2turn10search9turn11search0

**Inputs:**  
Statement balance and card limit.

**Worked example:**  
If a card limit is **EGP 40,000** and the statement balance would otherwise be **EGP 18,000**, utilization is **45%**. To bring it to **30%**, the statement balance must be **EGP 12,000**, so you need a **pre-statement payment of EGP 6,000**. To bring it to **10%**, you would need a statement balance of **EGP 4,000**, requiring **EGP 14,000** of pre-statement payment. citeturn18view1turn11search2turn10search9

**Monthly interest on a revolved balance**

**Formula:**  
`Approximate monthly interest = Revolving balance × Monthly finance charge`

**Purpose:**  
A fast way to estimate carrying cost. HSBC says interest is charged from the transaction date based on daily outstanding balance when the full outstanding amount is not paid by the due date, so this formula is an approximation for planning rather than an exact statement calculation. citeturn4view1turn3view3

**Inputs:**  
Revolving balance and card monthly finance charge.

**Worked example:**  
At a balance of **EGP 20,000** and HSBC’s published **4.42%** monthly charge, the first month’s interest is about:  
`20,000 × 4.42% = EGP 884`  
If you pay **EGP 1,000**, principal falls by only about **EGP 116** in that month before any other fees. That is why “just paying the minimum” barely moves the debt. citeturn7view1turn4view1

**Minimum-payment trap check**

**Formula:**  
`Principal reduction = Payment - Interest - Fees`  
If the result is very small, you are in a slow-amortization trap.

**Purpose:**  
Shows whether a payment is meaningfully shrinking debt.

**Inputs:**  
Actual payment, monthly interest, fees.

**Worked example:**  
Using the same **EGP 20,000** balance and **EGP 884** interest, a **EGP 1,000** payment reduces principal by only **EGP 116**. HSBC’s minimum-payment formula also includes installment monthly payments, over-limit amounts, insurance premiums, and then **EGP 50 or 5%** of the category balance, or total monthly interest and fees, whichever is higher. In practical terms, paying only the minimum is usually a very slow path. citeturn2view2turn4view1

**Installment payment formula**

**Formula:**  
`Monthly installment = P × r / (1 - (1 + r)^(-n))`

**Purpose:**  
Standard amortization formula for an installment or converted purchase plan.

**Inputs:**  
Principal amount `P`, monthly rate `r`, number of months `n`.

**Worked example:**  
If you convert **EGP 12,000** into a **12-month Flexi installment** at HSBC’s published **2.9% monthly** rate, the monthly payment is about **EGP 1,198**, and the total finance cost is roughly **EGP 2,380** over the year, before any product-specific quirks. A **12-month balance transfer** at **2.7% monthly** would be about **EGP 1,184** monthly and about **EGP 2,209** total finance cost, plus the published **1% processing fee** for the discounted-interest balance-transfer plan. citeturn6view4turn7view3

**Cash-advance cost**

**Formula:**  
`Cash advance fee = max(5% × amount, EGP 75)`  
`Plus interest from transaction date`

**Purpose:**  
To show why cash advance is a last-resort tool, not a budgeting tool.

**Inputs:**  
Cash amount withdrawn.

**Worked example:**  
A **EGP 5,000** cash advance incurs an immediate fee of **EGP 250**, and HSBC says there is **no interest-free period** for cash advances; interest runs from the transaction date until fully repaid. In other words, cash advance is the opposite of a 56-day free float. citeturn7view2turn4view1

**Liquidity runway**

**Formula:**  
`Liquidity runway in months = Liquid cash / Essential monthly spending`

**Purpose:**  
Measures how long you can cover core expenses without fresh borrowing. CFPB defines emergency savings as cash reserves set aside for unplanned expenses, and the user’s end-goal of three months is consistent with mainstream emergency-fund practice.

**Inputs:**  
Immediate cash and essential recurring expenses only.

**Worked example:**  
If liquid cash is **EGP 9,000** and essential monthly spending is **EGP 12,000**, liquidity runway is **0.75 months**. That is enough for a starter buffer, but not yet the desired three-month reserve. citeturn28search1turn28search13

**Float safety ratio**

**Formula:**  
`Float safety ratio = Ring-fenced cash reserved for card repayment / Upcoming statement balance`

**Purpose:**  
The critical measure for safe debit-credit-debit rotation.

**Inputs:**  
Cash already earmarked for the coming statement and the statement balance that will fall due.

**Worked example:**  
If the upcoming statement balance is **EGP 8,000** and you have only **EGP 5,500** consciously reserved to pay it, the ratio is **0.69**. That is **unsafe**. A safe float requires a ratio of at least **1.0**, and preferably a little above that so a posting delay or essential bill does not push the account overdue. citeturn3view3turn28search3

**Interest-free window**

**Formula:**  
`Free-float days = Days from purchase to statement close + Days from statement close to due date`  
Bounded by HSBC’s stated maximum.

**Purpose:**  
Shows why payment timing matters.

**Inputs:**  
Purchase date, statement-closing date, due date.

**Worked example:**  
If your statement closes on the **5th** and payment is due on the **31st**, then:  
- a purchase on the **6th** can sit almost a full cycle and then the due-date gap, reaching the HSBC maximum of roughly **56 days**;  
- a purchase on the **4th** only gets the short remaining days until the same statement closes, so the float can be closer to **27 days**.  
HSBC’s own FAQ also notes that a transaction posted after the statement-cycle cut will fall into the next cycle, which is why **posting date**, not just swipe date, matters. citeturn3view4turn19view0

**A practical spreadsheet layout**

A useful workbook needs only four tabs: **Budget**, **Debt**, **Statement Calendar**, and **KPI Dashboard**. CFPB provides public budgeting aids such as a spending tracker, bill calendar, prioritizing-bills tools, and a general budget worksheet, while digital-envelope systems like YNAB are commonly demonstrated in video explainers for handling credit-card spending without losing category control. Use the worksheet or app to make every card swipe reduce an equal amount from a budget category **and** increase the cash reserved for payment. citeturn28search0turn28search3turn28search6turn22search1turn22search9

## Which payoff method fits this goal

The best fit for this specific objective is **buffered avalanche plus utilization control**. That means: keep a starter liquidity floor, pay every minimum on time, direct extra principal to the **highest-cost** balance, and make targeted pre-statement payments so the reported utilization looks safer. This beats pure snowball when card rates are very high, because HSBC’s revolving card pricing is expensive enough that interest minimization matters a great deal, while iScore/FICO-style frameworks also reward lower revolving debt relative to limits. citeturn7view1turn7view2turn18view1turn11search13

Snowball still has a place. Academic work in the *Journal of Marketing Research* found evidence that consumers tend to prefer and persist with paying off smaller debts first because small wins create a stronger sense of progress. Related work summarized by Kellogg and later HBR coverage points in the same direction: snowball can improve motivation even when it is not mathematically cheapest. That makes snowball a valid fallback **only when adherence is your main problem**. If you know you will quit an avalanche plan after two painful months, a structured snowball may outperform a “perfect” but abandoned strategy. citeturn12search2turn12search13turn12search16

At the same time, credit-card research shows that minimum payments are powerful anchors. NBER work using a large credit-card dataset found that many accounts pay at or near the minimum, and that salient minimum-payment cues materially affect repayment behavior. A more recent NBER field experiment found that nudges can reduce enrollment in minimum-autopay setups, but debt itself does not necessarily fall if liquidity is tight. The lesson is important: **behavioral hacks matter, but liquidity still rules**. If your cashflow is genuinely too tight, optimism about payment behavior will not substitute for a real spending surplus. citeturn23view0turn24view0turn23view2turn24view2

The comparison below synthesizes the math, the behavioral evidence, and the local scoring logic.

| Method | What it optimizes | Main advantage | Main weakness | Best use case | Risk level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avalanche | Lowest total interest cost | Fastest mathematical debt reduction | Can feel slow if biggest/highest-rate balance is emotionally unpleasant | High-rate card debt with disciplined user | Low |
| Snowball | Motivation and visible wins | Quick balance closures can increase adherence | Usually costs more interest | User struggles to stick to plans | Medium |
| Fixed-payment | Simplicity and routine | Easy to automate and repeat monthly | Can be suboptimal if not targeted | Irregular attention span, desire for low-friction habit | Low to medium |
| Utilization-first | Better reported revolving profile | Can improve statement balance optics before application | May delay deeper payoff if overused | Pre-application month or when one card is heavily over limit/over 30% | Low |
| Cash-buffer-first | Short-term resilience | Reduces relapse into new borrowing after surprises | Slower immediate debt payoff | No emergency cushion, volatile income | Low |
| Buffered avalanche plus utilization control | Balanced cost, stability, and lender-facing cleanliness | Best mix for this objective | Requires calendar discipline | Preparing for loan application over a few months | Low |

This comparison rests on the NBER and JMR behavioral findings, iScore/FICO utilization logic, and CFPB emergency-savings guidance. citeturn23view0turn23view1turn23view2turn12search2turn18view1turn11search2turn28search1

There is also a tactic you should treat very cautiously: **installment-first for “credit mix” optics**. Egypt’s iScore education says credit mix contributes to scoring, and CIB’s local explainer says good management of different kinds of accounts can help. But mix is a **smaller factor** than payment history and outstanding debt. Paying 2.7%–3.0% monthly on a new plan just to manufacture “mix” is usually a bad trade if you still carry 4.42% revolving debt. The exception is a **necessary** purchase placed on a true 0% plan with low total fees and a payment small enough that it does not weaken cashflow. citeturn14view0turn18view1turn7view3turn30view0

## Action plan for the next month

The next month should be treated as a **stabilization month**, not an optimization game. Start by making a full inventory of every balance, statement date, due date, limit, salary date, and automatic bill. CFPB’s bill-calendar and spending-tracker tools are designed exactly for this kind of cashflow mapping. The purpose is simple: you cannot run a safe card float unless you know when money enters, when statements cut, and when obligations leave. citeturn28search0turn28search3turn28search9

If any HSBC card is already revolving, **stop using that same card for new purchases immediately**. HSBC’s terms say interest is charged if the full outstanding amount is not paid by the due date, and interest is charged from the transaction date based on the daily outstanding balance. Public consumer discussions often describe the bad version of this behavior as “credit card float”: paying last month’s life with this month’s income. That community warning is anecdotal, but it aligns with the official economics of a revolved card. Use debit for daily spend—or, only if you already have one, a separate card that you can and will pay in full every cycle. citeturn3view3turn4view1turn21search0turn21search8

Set up the payment infrastructure carefully. HSBC allows a standing instruction from an HSBC current or savings account to settle the outstanding amount **in full or in part** on the due date. If there are insufficient funds, the card may become overdue and a late-payment fee may apply. HSBC also warns that a standing instruction can still be debited even if you made manual repayments within the prior three business days. For most people in payoff mode, the best operational setup is **autopay the minimum as a fail-safe**, then make your real planned payment manually a few days earlier; for a pure full-pay card backed by stable cash, full-statement autopay is cleaner. citeturn3view3turn9view0

A practical monthly budget structure should be **zero-based**: every pound gets assigned a job to debt, essentials, savings, or controlled leisure before the month starts. Fidelity’s explainer describes zero-based budgeting as assigning every dollar a job, and CFPB’s toolkit offers matching tracking tools. The important adaptation for credit cards is that every card purchase must immediately reduce a spending category **and** increase the amount mentally or digitally reserved for repayment. That is how you get card rewards or float without lying to yourself about available cash. citeturn28search2turn28search0turn22search1

An illustrative monthly budget for a user with **EGP 20,000 net cash inflow**, **EGP 24,000 gross income**, an existing installment of **EGP 1,000**, a card limit of **EGP 40,000**, and a revolved card balance of **EGP 18,000** would look like this:

| Category | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Core essentials | EGP 12,000 |
| Utilities, transport, phone, admin | EGP 2,000 |
| Limited leisure | EGP 1,000 |
| Existing installment | EGP 1,000 |
| Revolved card payment | EGP 3,000 |
| Starter cash buffer build | EGP 1,000 |
| Total | EGP 20,000 |

At **EGP 3,000** per month and no new charges on that revolved card, the balance would fall from **EGP 18,000** to roughly **EGP 11,090** in three months at a 4.42% monthly finance charge. That is not debt freedom, but it is a meaningful visible improvement in both utilization and monthly interest drag. citeturn7view1turn4view1

The weekly rhythm matters more than the monthly slogan. A workable next-month plan is:

| Week | Main action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Opening week | Build the bill calendar, list balances and limits, stop new spending on any revolved card, set minimum-autopay fail-safes | Prevents accidental delinquency and exposes true cashflow timing |
| Following week | Cancel or pause low-value subscriptions, set hard weekly caps for groceries, transport, and leisure, and begin a daily spend log | Creates the surplus that makes the plan real |
| Middle week | Make the main avalanche payment on payday; if projected statement utilization is too high, make a pre-statement payment before the close date | Reduces both interest cost and lender-visible card stress |
| Closing week | Review the fresh statement, confirm no late fees, archive payment confirmation, and update the KPI sheet | Builds a clean three-month record |

This sequence is grounded in HSBC’s payment mechanics, iScore-style risk logic, and CFPB budgeting tools. citeturn3view3turn18view1turn14view0turn28search0turn28search3

The **KPIs to track weekly** should be few and unforgiving: on-time status, total revolving balance, statement utilization, required debt service, starter buffer level, and leisure spend versus cap. A clean dashboard is better than a complicated one. If you have only one card and it is in revolving mode, your KPI target is simple: **zero new purchases on that card** until the account is back in full-pay status or you have written confirmation about grace restoration. citeturn4view1turn3view3

## Consistency plan for the following quarter

The following quarter should be about **repeatability and clean reporting**, not speed for its own sake. Month one is for stabilizing, month two is for proving consistency, and month three is for polishing the file before any application. The ideal picture by the end of the quarter is: three consecutive clean payment cycles, lower reported revolving debt, a visible cash cushion, and no unnecessary hard inquiries. CIB’s explainer on Egypt’s scoring logic specifically warns that too many new applications can signal difficulty, and HSBC’s own missed-payment language is explicit about negative effects on creditworthiness and credit-bureau reporting. citeturn14view0turn3view3

If you already have a legitimate installment obligation, just pay it perfectly for the full quarter and let that history accumulate naturally. If you do **not** have one, the safest default is **not** to manufacture a new interest-bearing installment merely to look more “bankable.” Egypt’s scoring logic treats payment history and debt load as more important than credit mix, and HSBC’s installment pricing is not trivial: its current tariff sheet shows roughly **2.7% monthly** for discounted-interest balance transfer, **2.9% monthly** for Flexi installment, and **3.0% monthly** for cash installment, with various processing or early-settlement fees depending on product. That is expensive optics. citeturn14view0turn7view3

The one exception is a **necessary planned purchase** that you already intended to make anyway, placed on a **genuine** low-cost installment. HSBC’s public materials show both ordinary purchase-installment options and partner-led installment plans. Some partner plans are true 0% offers, such as certain Noon offers, but others can still charge substantial administrative fees—for example, one Tradeline example is advertised as 12 months with a **13% administrative fee**. So the correct test is **all-in cost**, not headline rate. If the total fee is low, the purchase is necessary, and the monthly installment is comfortably affordable, that can be reasonable. Otherwise, skip it. citeturn29search1turn30view0

Balance transfer deserves a similarly narrow use case. HSBC advertises a **0% balance-transfer plan for the first three months** with no processing fee, but after that the normal card rate applies. That can help only if your payoff capacity is strong enough to clear the transferred balance inside the promotional window. If not, the transfer can simply delay the pain and then return you to expensive revolving interest. Use it only when the math is explicit and the exit path is certain. citeturn30view1turn7view3

Over the quarter, the cleanest ethical preparation steps are straightforward. Pull your iScore report **once** to check for errors, then avoid repeated applications and repeated unnecessary bureau pulls. Keep salary deposits and account activity orderly. If your employer can genuinely transfer salary and HSBC’s salary-transfer finance path fits your real employment situation, that can strengthen a later application because it matches HSBC’s published eligibility structure; but do not create a fake transfer arrangement or submit inaccurate income letters. HSBC explicitly requires recent income letters and transfer letters for the relevant products. citeturn17view0turn14view0turn2view0

The tactical application window is usually the week after a **low-utilization statement** has generated, not the day before it. Because statement-cycle postings matter, and because reported balances often reflect the statement snapshot rather than your later manual payment history, it is sensible to make a pre-statement payment in the month you expect to apply so the printed balance is lower. That is an inference from statement-cycle mechanics and utilization-based risk logic, not a published HSBC underwriting rule. The key point is to make the file look cleaner **honestly**, by owing less at the statement date, not by borrowing elsewhere. citeturn19view0turn18view1turn11search2

The tactics to avoid are just as important. Do **not** use cash advance for “temporary liquidity”: HSBC charges **5% with a minimum of EGP 75**, and there is **no interest-free period**. Do **not** chain one card to another or use new debt to make minimum payments in a loop. Do **not** assume a merchant installment is harmless just because it says “0%.” Do **not** apply to several banks at once to test which one approves. And do **not** keep spending on the same card you are revolving. Those are exactly the behaviors most likely to increase cost, increase fragility, or create a weaker bank-facing picture. citeturn7view2turn4view1turn30view0turn30view1turn14view0

The bottom line is simple. If your real objective is HSBC eligibility in Egypt, the highest-probability ethical route is: **pay on time every time, lower the revolving balances that are most visible and most expensive, keep a starter cash buffer so you do not relapse into new card debt, use the 56-day loop only on a true full-pay card, and avoid any tactic whose success depends on future borrowing to clean up present borrowing**. That is the strategy most aligned with HSBC’s published product structure, Egypt’s credit-reporting logic, and the economics of high monthly card rates. citeturn2view0turn18view2turn18view1turn7view1turn3view4
